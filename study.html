Study Tips

Reading: Information & Ideas

Central Ideas

What to look for:

  • Identify the author's overall message rather than individual details.
  • Pay attention to repeated ideas or themes, the introduction and conclusion, and the tone of the passage.
  • How do the supporting details work together to develop one central point?

Strategies:

  • Summarize the passage in your own words before looking at the answer choices.
  • Eliminate answers that are too specific or too generalized.

Common wording:

  • Which choice best states the central idea?
  • What is the main point of the passage?
  • Which statement best summarizes the text?

Common traps:

  • Be cautious of extreme language. (all, none, certain, never, always, only, etc.)
  • The answer choice simply repeats words from the passage without expressing the text's overall meaning.
Details

What to look for:

  • These questions are answered directly by the passage. Find what information is needed by reading the question.
  • Once the relevant lines are located, read a few sentences before and after to understand the context.

Strategies:

  • Go back to the passage instead of relying on memory
  • Read the surrounding context carefully
  • Choose the answer that is most directly supported by the text.

Common wording:

  • According to the passage...
  • Which choice best describes...?
  • The passage indicates that...
  • Which statement is supported by the text?
  • Based on the passage, which choice is true

Common traps:

  • Avoid answers that are almost correct but change one key detail
  • Avoid answers that include information not mentioned in the passage
  • Do not rely on outside knowledge as textual evidence
  • Be cautious of extreme language. (all, none, certain, never, always, only, etc.)
Inferences

What to look for:

  • The correct answer will not be stated word-for-word but must be directly supported by the information in the passage.

Strategies:

  • Base your answer only on the evidence provided in the text.
  • Combine context clues when necessary to draw a logical conclusion.
  • Ask what must be true, not what could be true.

Common wording:

  • Which choice can most reasonably be inferred?
  • The passage suggests that...
  • Which conclusion is best supported by the passage?
  • The author would most likely agree that...
  • It can reasonably be concluded that...

Common traps:

  • Assumptions beyond the passage.
  • Choosing an answer that is possible but unsupported
  • Be cautious of extreme language. (all, none, certain, never, always, only, etc.)
Command of Evidence: Textual

What to look for:

  • Identify the evidence in the answer choices that most directly supports the claim or conclusion of the text.
  • Focus on the option that provides the most complete support, not just one that mentions similar words.

Strategies:

  • Find the claim or conclusion first. This is usually found at the end of the passage.
  • Break the claim down into two pieces of information to compare against the answer choices.
  • Elimanate any answers that don't support both parts of the claim

Common wording:

  • Which quotation best supports...?
  • Which choice provides the strongest evidence...?
  • Which lines best support the claim?

Common traps:

  • Picking evidence because it contains familiar words from the text
  • Choosing evidence that is related in topic but doesn't directly support the claim
  • Selecting a true statement that answers only part of the claim.
  • Be cautious of extreme language. (all, none, certain, never, always, only, etc.)
Command of Evidence: Quantitative

What to look for:

  • Carefully examine the graph, table, or chart, including titles, labels, axes, units, and trends.
  • Find the claim, conclusion, or example in the text.
  • Compare the data with the answer choices to ensure the statement accurately reflects the claim, conclusion, or example presented.

Strategies:

  • Read the graph and passage before the answers
  • Verify numbers rather than estimating
  • Pay attention to units and scales
  • Make sure both the data and accompanying text are supported in the answer choice.

Common wording:

  • Which choice best uses the data from the graph/table?
  • Based on the graph/table...
  • Which choice accurately best supports the claim/conclusion?
  • The data suggest that...

Common traps:

  • Misreading axes or units
  • Choosing an answer that sounds reasonable but does not accurately match the data on the graph/table.
  • Be cautious of extreme language. (all, none, certain, never, always, only, etc.)

Reading: Craft & Structure

Words in Context

What to look for:

  • Determine how the word is being used in context, not its most common dictionary definition
  • Read the sentence containing the word and the sentences before and after it for clues about the author's intended meaning.

Strategies:

  • Replace the word with each answer choice and see which one fits naturally in context.
  • Look for synonyms, definitions, examples, or contrasts provided by the surrounding sentences.
  • Try to find familiar word parts (prefix, base, suffix) or similar sounding words when unsure about the vocabulary terms

Common wording:

  • As used in the text, the word "___" most nearly means...
  • Which choice best defines "___" as it is used in the passage?
  • What is the most percise and logial word in the context of the passage?

Common traps:

  • Choosing the most familiar definition instead of the contextual one
  • Ignoring the surrounding sentences
  • Selecting an answer choice just because the word sounds sophisticated or the most complex
Text Purpose

What to look for:

  • Identify why the author included a particular sentence, paragraph, or example.
  • Pay attention to how the author is introducing a topic, providing evidence, illustrating an idea, acknowledging a counterargument, explaining a concept, or drawing a conclusion.

Strategies:

  • Focus on the function of the selected text rather than its content/topic alone.
  • Think about how different parts of the text contribute to the author's overall argument or discussion.
  • Pay attention to tone and word choice. How did the author decide to talk about the main topic?

Common wording:

  • The primary purpose of the text is to...
  • What is the function of the underlined sentence/paragraph in the overall text purpose?

Common traps:

  • Choosing an answer that describes what the sentence says instead of why it was included
  • Selecting an answer that is too broad (generalization) or too narrow (details)
  • not keeping the overall text purpose in mind when answering part-to-whole relationship questions
Text Structure

What to look for:

  • Notice how the author builds the ideas passage
  • Common structures include problem–solution, cause–effect, compare–contrast, chronological order, claim–evidence, question–answer, or general idea followed by specific examples.
  • Pay attention to transition words that signal shifts in organization.

Strategies:

  • Briefly summarize the role of each paragraph or section, then identify the overall organizational pattern
  • Find the transition words like however, therefore, for example, and in contrast.

Common wording:

  • How is the text organized?
  • Which choice best describes the structure of the passage?
  • Which choice best describes the structure of the underlined sentence/paragraph in the passage as a whole?

Common traps:

  • Focusing on the topic instead of the organization,
  • Overlooking shifts in the author's ideas or transition words
  • Choosing a structure that describes only one part of the passage, rather than the whole.
Cross-Text Connections

What to look for:

  • Read each text independently first.
  • Identify each author's main claim, purpose, tone, and evidence before comparing them.
  • Look for areas the authors are in agreement, disagreement, or have different perspectives on the same topic.

Strategies:

  • Summarize the main idea of each text before comparing them.
  • Pay attention to differences in what each author emphasises, not just whether they generally agree or disagree.
  • Think of these questions like a venn diagram. Find the information in common and what's different.

Common wording:

  • How would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the conclusion/claim in Text 1?
  • Which statement would both authors most likely agree/disagree with?
  • Which choice best describes the relationship between the two texts?

Common traps:

  • Assuming the authors have identical views because they discuss the same topic
  • Overlooking subtle differences in opinion or emphasis
  • Choosing an answer supported by only one of the two texts instead of both.
  • Be cautious of extreme language. (all, none, certain, never, always, only, etc.)

Writing: Standard English Conventions

Boundaries

What to look for:

  • Look for the punctuation that changes between answer choices
  • Determine whether the ideas on each side are complete sentences, incomplete phrases, or additional information.

Strategies:

  • Identify the clause on each side of the blank before looking at the answer choices
  • Use a semicolon or period only to separate two complete sentences
  • Use a colon only after a complete sentence to introduce a list, direct quote, or explanation
  • Eliminate any choice that creates a fragment, run-on, or comma splice.
  • Skip over information between two commas to test if the clause is essential or nonessential to the sentence's meaning

Common wording:

  • Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Common traps:

  • Comma splices, sentence fragments, run-on sentences
  • Colons placed after incomplete thoughts
  • Incorrect punctuation around nonessential information (commas and dashes)
Form, Structure, and Sense

What to look for:

  • Look for changes in verb tense, subject-verb agreement, and noun-pronoun agreement
  • Pay ateention to sentence structure and wording that affects the meaning of the sentence (modifier placement)

Strategies:

  • Read the entire sentence before choosing an answer
  • Predict which grammatical form the sentence requires before checking the answer choices
  • Make sure the completed sentence is both grammatically correct and logically clear.

Common wording:

  • Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Common traps:

  • Match a verb to the nearest noun instead of the actual subject of the sentence
  • Ignoring clues elsewhere in the sentence that establish the correct verb tense or pronoun
  • Choose a word because it sounds familiar rather than because it fits grammatically
  • Not reading the whole sentence when checking for clairty

Writing: Expression of Ideas

Transitions

What to look for:

  • Look for the relationship between the ideas before and after the blank
  • Determine how the ideas before and after the blank are organized

Strategies:

  • Read the whole passage before looking at the answer choice
  • Decide whether the author is adding information, contrasting ideas, showing cause and effect, giving an example, or describing a sequence.
  • Choose the transition that best reflects the organizational structure

Common wording:

  • Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

Common traps:

  • Picking a transition because sounds familiar or sophisticated
  • Incorrectly identifying the relationship between the ideas in the passage
  • Not knowing the transition word or the relationship the transition word indicates
Rhetorical Synthesis

What to look for:

  • Look for the writer's goal, which is stated in the question
  • Focus only on the information needed to accomplish that goal, whether it is summarizing, comparing, supporting a claim, describing a trend, or introducing a topic.

Strategies:

  • Read the question and identify exactly what the writer wants to accomplish
  • Choose the answer that directly fulfills the goal without adding unnecessary details or outside information.
  • Scan the notes for only the relevant information if choosing between answer choices

Common wording:

  • Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish the writer's goal?

Common traps:

  • Try to include as many details as possible instead of selecting only the information needed for the stated purpose
  • Choosing an answer that is factually correct but doesn't accomplish the whole task
  • Getting distracted by interesting details that are irrelevant.

Math

Algebra

Linear Equations One Variable

What to look for:

  • Isolate the variable by performing inverse operations
  • Watch for parentheses, fractions, and variables on both sides of the equation.
  • If solving algebraically looks tedious or involves multiple fractions, decimals, radiacls, it's often faster to solve in Desmos.

Strategies:

  • Distribute and simplify by combining like terms
  • Check your answer by substituting it back into the equation.
  • Desmos Tip: Enter the equation with x as the variable and find the x-intercept (the value of x where the line crosses the X-axis)
  • Desmos Tip: Graph y=(left side of the equation)-(right side of the equation) and find the x-intercept.(the value of x where the line crosses the X-axis)

Common wording:

  • Solve for x
  • What is the value/solution of x?
  • Which value satisfies the equation?

Common traps:

  • Sign errors (forgetting a negative sign)
  • Forgetting to distribute or combining unlike terms (x with X^2)
  • Arithmetic mistakes when moving terms across the equals sign.
  • Inputting the equation into Desmos incorrectly
  • Incorrectly identifying the x-intercept in Desmos
Linear Equations Two Variables

What to look for:

  • Identify the relationship between the variables by substituting values or analyzing the graph
  • If the problem asks whether a point lies on a line or asks for values from a graph, Desmos can often verify the answer much faster than manual calculations.

Strategies:

  • Substitute carefully
  • Verify both coordinates satisfy the equation (x value and y value)
  • Rewrite equations in slope-intercept form (y=mx+b)
  • Desmos Tip: Graph the equation and check whether a given point lies on the line
  • Desmos Tip: Use the table feature to test coordinate points (x,y) quickly.
  • Desmos Tip: The x-intercept of the line is the solution for x, and the y-intercept of the line is the solution for y

Common wording:

  • Which ordered pair satisfies the equation?
  • Find the value of y when x=...
  • Which point lies on the line?
  • Write the equation of the line that...

Common traps:

  • Swapping x- and y-coordinates
  • Substituting the wrong value or substituting for the wrong veriable
  • Choosing a point that satisfies only the x value or only the y value
  • Inputting the equation or points into Desmos incorrectly
  • Incorrectly identifying the intercept in Desmos
Linear Functions

What to look for:

  • Identify the slope (constant rate of change)and y-intercept from equations, tables, graphs, or word problems.
  • Whenever the problem involves a graph or asks you to compare functions, Desmos is often the quickest way to visualize and verify your work.

Strategies:

  • Calculate the slope and intercepts carefully
  • Recognize that linear functions have a constant rate of change (slope)
  • Desmos Tip: Graph the function ( f(x)=2x-6 ) to identify slope and intercepts
  • Desmos Tip: Graph multiple functions simultaneously to compare them.
  • Desmos Tip: Define the function using f(x)=..., then let Desmos do the work by evaluating expressions like f(2), f(-5), f(a), f(2x), or even f(x)-f(2x)

Common wording:

  • What is the slope/intercept of the function?
  • Which equation represents the graph?
  • What is the value of f(x) when....?

Common traps:

  • Confusing slope with the y-intercept in y=mx+b form
  • Misreading the graph in DesmosSubstituting into the function incorrectly when evaluating expressions like f(2x) or f(x+h).
Linear Inequalities

What to look for:

  • Solve as you would an equation, remembering to reverse (flip) the inequality sign when multiplying or dividing by a negative
  • If you're unsure about the solution set or graph, use Desmos for verification.

Strategies:

  • Isolate the variable, test a value if necessary
  • Desmos Tip: Graph the inequality. The shaded region of the graph is the range of possible solutions
  • Desmos Tip: Use the shaded region of the graph to confirm whether values/points satisfy the inequality.

Common wording:

  • Solve the inequality
  • Which value/point satisfies the inequality?
  • Which graph represents the solution to the inequality?

Common traps:

  • Forgetting to reverse the inequality sign when multiplying or dividing by a negative
  • Graphing the inequality incorrectly or using the wrong inequality sign
  • Confusing dotted (value not included in the solution) versus solid (value is included in the solution) line in Desmos
Systems of Linear Equations

What to look for:

  • Find the point where both equations are true (where they intersect/cross)
  • Unless the Algebra is especially simple, systems are one of the best opportunities to use Desmos
  • Recognize when lines are parallel (no solution) or identical (infinitely many solutions).

Strategies:

  • Choose substitution or elimination only if it's quicker than graphing
  • Simplify equations before solving (combine like terms), and verify your solution in both equations.
  • Desmos Tip: Graph both equations and use the intersection/crossing point as your solution.
  • Desmos Tip: If the lines never intersect (parallel/ same slope) the system has no solution
  • Desmos Tip: If the lines overlap completely (same line), there are infinitely many solutions to the system.

Common wording:

  • Solve the system
  • What is the solution to the system?
  • At what point/value do the lines intersect?
  • Which ordered pair satisfies the system of equations?

Common traps:

  • Arithmetic mistakes during elimination or substitution
  • Forgetting to check he solutions in both equations
  • Misinterpreting parallel or identical lines.
  • Inputting the equation(s) into Desmos incorrectly
  • Looking for the intercepts of the individual lines instead of where the lines intersect with one another

Advanced Math

Quadratic Equations & Functions

What to look for:

  • Look for quadratic equations in standard, vertex, or factored form
  • Pay attention to the x and y intercepts, vertex, maximum or minimum values, and questions involving roots, zeros, or solutions.

Strategies:

  • Identify which form of the quadratic is given
  • Factor when possible, use the quadratic formula when necessary, and use vertex form to quickly identify the maximum or minimum value
  • Desmos Tip: graph the equation to find x-intercepts, y-intercept, the vertex (minimum/maximum), axis of symmetry, or compare multiple answer choices by graphing the lines
  • Desmos Tip: The x solution(s) are where the line crosses the x-axis. The y solution is where the line crosses the y-axis

Common wording:

  • Which value of x is a solution?
  • Which value/point satisfies the given equation/function?
  • What is the vertex/minimum/maximum of the graph?
  • Which equation represents the graph?

Common traps:

  • Only finding one solution for x when there are two solutions
  • Forgetting to set the equation equal to zero before factoring or using the quadratic formula
  • Confuse the x-coordinate of the vertex with the maximum or minimum value
  • Sign errors when factoring and solving
  • Inputting the equation into Desmos incorrectly or checking the wrong part of the graph
Exponential Equations & Functions

What to look for:

  • Identify variables in the exponent, repeated multiplication, exponential growth or decay
  • Look for equations written in the form a(b)x

Strategies:

  • Determine whether the situation represents growth or decay
  • Identify the initial value, growth factor, and time period
  • growth/decay = initial amount(1 ± rate as a decimal)# of time periods
  • Desmos Tip: graph the function or create a table with at leat two points to compare values and estimate solutions

Common wording:

  • Which function models the situation?
  • What is the value of the function when...

Common traps:

  • Confusing the initial value with the growth factor
  • Incorrectly identifying whether the situation requires growth or decay
Polynomial Functions

What to look for:

  • Look for higher-degree equations (highest exponent is 3 or larger)
  • Factors, solutions/zeros/roots

Strategies:

  • Factor or distribute the given equation when possible
  • Use the Zero Product Property (set each factor to zero) to find solutions
  • Analyze the degree (highest exponent) to identify the maximum number of solutions to the equation
  • Desmos Tip: graph the polynomial to locate solutions/zeros/roots

Common wording:

  • Which value is a solution/zero/root of the function?
  • Which expression is a factor?
  • Which point satisfies the given equation?

Common traps:

  • Repeated solutions/zeros/roots
  • Confusing factors with zeros
  • Forgetting that some polynomials cannot be factored easily
Rational Functions

What to look for:

  • Look for fractions containing variables
  • Equations that require multiplying by a denominator to solve

Strategies:

  • Identify values that make the denominator zero before solving (holes/undefined)
  • Multiply both sides by the least common denominator when appropriate
  • Desmos Tip: graph the function to identify vertical and horizontal asymptotes(holes) and intercepts/solutions/zeros/roots

Common wording:

  • Which value is the equation undefined?
  • What is the solution to the function?
  • Which graph represents the function?
  • Solve for x

Common traps:

  • The denominator cannot equal zero. The values that make the denominator zero are the holes/asymptotes
  • Canceling terms incorrectly
  • Overlooking holes created by common factors.
Equivalent Expressions

What to look for:

  • Look for opportunities to simplify, factor, distribute, rewrite, or identify expressions that are mathematically equivalent.

Strategies:

  • Rewrite expressions using exponent rules, the distributive property, or factoring
  • If the question doesn't ask for the values of the varables, substitute some numbers into the expression. The Equivalent expressions will have the same answer/value
  • Desmos Tip: graph the expression and check each answer choice to find which expression is the same line

Common wording:

  • Which expression is equivalent to...
  • Rewrite the expression in simplest form

Common traps:

  • Distributing incorrectly
  • Combining unlike terms
  • Incorrectly applying exponent rules
  • Assuming two expressions are not eqivalent because they look different
Transformations

What to look for:

  • Look for graphs or equations that have been shifted, reflected, stretched, compressed, or translated
  • Pay attention to changes inside the function notation (ex: f(x+2) ) and outside the function notation (ex: f(x)+2 )

Strategies:

  • Start with the given function and identify each transformation one at a time
  • Changes inside parentheses affect x-values, while changes outside parentheses affect y-values
  • Desmos Tip: graph the given function and the transformed function together to visualize shifts, reflections, and stretches

Common wording:

  • Which equation represents the graph
  • The function f(x) is..(describes tranformation). Which of the following correctly defines the function of f?

Common traps:

  • Confusing horizontal and vertical shifts
  • Reversing the direction of horizontal translations (subtracting from x moves the graph right, adding moves the graph left)
  • Mixing up reflections across the x-axis (-f(x)) and y-axis (f(-x))
  • Overlooking stretches and compressions when comparing graphs

Geometry

Area & Volume

What to look for:

  • Look for figures that require finding area, perimeter, surface area, volume, or missing dimensions
  • Pay close attention to the units and whether the figure is two-dimensional (area and perimeter) or three-dimensional (surface area and volume)

Strategies:

  • Check whether the problem asks for an area, perimeter, surface area, or volume
  • Identify the shape(s) involved and write down the appropriate formula from the reference sheet before solving
  • Break complex figures into simpler shapes when necessary
  • Desmos Tips: use the calculator for complex operations with multiple steps

Common wording:

  • What is the area of...?
  • What is the volume of...?
  • What is the length/width/height of...?"

Common traps:

  • Using the wrong formula to solve
  • Forgetting to square or cube dimensions when solving
  • Confusing area (2D) with volume (3D)
  • Overlook that a missing dimension must be found before using the formula
Lines, Angles, & Triangles

What to look for:

  • Parallel or perpendicular lines
  • Transversals (a line crossing through parallel lines)
  • Angle relationships and triangle properties
  • Pythagorean Theorem
  • Special right triangles (30°-60°-90° and 45°-45°-90°)
  • Similar or proportional triangles.

Strategies:

  • Draw or label missing information directly on a diagram
  • When a line crosses through parallel lines (transversal), all the small angles are the same measure, all the large angles are the same measure, and (big angle + small angle = 180°
  • Look for similar triangles and proportional relationships whenever triangles have the same angles
  • Desmos Tip: graph lines or triangles when coordinates are given
  • Desmos Tip: use the distance formula, or verify slopes to determine whether lines are parallel or perpendicular

Common wording:

  • What is the value of x?
  • Which angle measure is...
  • What is the length of...?
  • Which statement must be true?

Common traps:

  • Assuming figures are drawn to scale
  • Forgetting angle relationships created by parallel lines
  • Confusing the Pythagorean Theorem with special right triangle ratios
  • Missing similar triangles hidden within larger diagrams
Circles

What to look for:

  • Look for dimensions like the radius, diameter, arcs, sectors, central angles, inscribed angles, circumference, or area
  • Identify the relationship between chords and tangents to the circle
  • Look for th equation of a circle (the equation has a x2 and a y2)

Strategies:

  • Remember that the diameter is twice the radius
  • Inscribed angles measure half their intercepted arc
  • Tangent lines are perpendicular to the radius at the point it intersects the circle
  • Desmos Tip: graph circle equations to visualize the center and radius, verify intersections, or compare answer choice
  • Desmos Tip: use the top and bottom coordinate points on the circle along with the distance function to find the diameter
  • Desmos Tip: use the top and bottom coordinate points on the circle along with the midpoint function to find the center of the circle

Common wording:

  • What is the radius/diameter/angle?
  • What is the circumference/area?
  • What is the measure of the arc?
  • What is the area of the sector?
  • Which equation represents the circle?

Common traps:

  • Confusing the radius for the diameter
  • Forgetting that tangents are perpendicular to the radius
  • Incorrectly applying the circumference or area formula
  • Not recognizing the equation of a circle in standard form (x-h)2+(y-k)2=r2
Trigonometry

What to look for:

  • Look for right triangles
  • Identify angle measures, side lengths, and word problems involving angles of elevation or depression
  • Look for sine, cosine, and tangent relationships between sides and angles of right triangles

Strategies:

  • Identify the reference angle θ
  • Label the opposite, adjacent, and hypotenuse for the given angle before choosing a trig ratio
  • Use inverse trig functions when solving for an angle given the ratio of two sides
  • Recognize when using special right triangles is faster than solving with trigonometry
  • Desmos Tip: evaluate trig expressions in Degree Mode and use the calculator for inverse sine, cosine, or tangent to find unknown angles

Common wording:

  • What is the value of x?
  • What is the measure of θ?
  • What is the length of the side?
  • What is the measure of the angle
  • What is the sine/cosine/tangent of...?

Common traps:

  • Mixing up the opposite and adjacent sides or forgetting the hypotenuse is always the longest side
  • Choosing the wrong trig ratio for the given sides and angle
  • Use trigonometry when a simpler geometric relationship would solve the problem more quickly

Problem Solving & Data Analysis

Ratios, Rates, & Proportions

What to look for:

  • Look for ratios, proportions, scale factors, similar figures, or word problems comparing quantities.
  • Identify unit rates (distance/time)

Strategies:

  • Identify what quantities are being compared and keep the units consistent
  • Set up a proportion or unit rate before solving
  • Desmos Tip: solve proportions by graphing the equation (always use x as the variable) and finding the x-intercept

Common wording:

  • What is the ratio of..?
  • What is the unit rate?
  • If..., then...

Common traps:

  • Comparing the wrong values or not keeping units consistent
  • Forgetting to convert units when needed
  • Solve the proportion correctly but answering with the wrong value
Percentages

What to look for:

  • Look for percent increase, percent decrease, or percent of a quantity
  • Identify discounts, tax, or percent change.

Strategies:

  • Change percentages to decimals before calculating with them
  • Use the percent change formula when comparing old and new values ((new value - original value)/ original value)
  • Desmos Tip: calculate percentages with the % of function (shift+5)

Common wording:

  • What percent of...
  • By what percent did...?
  • What is the percent increase/decrease?
  • After a discount..
  • Including/not including tax..."

Common traps:

  • Confusing percent increase with percentages
  • Switching the new value and original value when calculating percent change
  • Forgetting to convert a percent to a decimal before using it in a calculation
Distribution & Center of Spread

What to look for:

  • Look for mean, median, mode, range, and standard deviation
  • Identify questions comparing data sets

Strategies:

  • Calculate the average when necessary (sum of all the numbers/ number of numbers)
  • Put data in ascending order before finding the median (middle number)
  • If two numbers are the "middle numbers" find there average to get the set's median
  • Identify the largest and smallest data point to find the range (biggest number - smallest number)
  • Data that is more spread out has a higher standard deviation
  • Desmos Tip: Use the built-in statistics functions to calculate measures of center and spread quickly

Common wording:

  • What is the mean/median/mode/range of the data?
  • Which data set has the greater mean/median/mode/range?
  • Which statement is supported by the data?

Common traps:

  • Solving for the incorrect part of the average formula
  • Not putting the data in order before finding the median
  • Missing data points
  • Assuming that two data sets with the same mean/median have the same standard deviation/spread
Two-Variable Data & Scatterplots

What to look for:

  • Look for scatterplotsliand their lines of best fit
  • Positive or negative trends, clusters, and outliers in the data

Strategies:

  • Identify the direction and strength of the correlation/trend before answering
  • Use the line of best fit to predict values rather than estimate them
  • Desmos Tip: graph the data, use regression (y₁ ~ mx₁ + b) to create a best-fit line, and estimate values from the graph

Common wording:

  • Which statement best describes the relationship?
  • What is the predicted value?
  • Which graph best represents the data?
  • Which equation best represents the line of best fit?

Common traps:

  • Using actual data points instead of the line of best fit
  • Misidentifying the type of equation needed to create a line of best fit (linear, quadratic, exponential)
Probability

What to look for:

  • Simple and compound probability
  • Independent versus dependent events
  • Finding probability from tables and graphs.

Strategies:

  • Identify the total number of possible outcomes before calculating
  • Identify the desired outcomes
  • Determine whether events are independent (with replacement) or dependent (without replacement)
  • Decide whether to multiply (AND) or add (OR) probabilities
  • Desmos Tip: use the calculator for combinations, permutations, and probability calculations

Common wording:

  • What is the probability that...?
  • If an item is selected at random..
  • What is the likelihood...?

Common traps:

  • Performing the incorrect operation for the situation (add/multiply)
  • Confusing independent and dependent events
Data Inferences & Margin of Error

What to look for:

  • Survey results and random samples
  • Populations, confidence intervals, margin of error, or estimates based on collected data

Strategies:

  • Determine whether the conclusion applies to the sample or the whole population
  • Add and subtract the margin of error from the sample's percentage to get the range of probability for the whole population
  • When a random sample of a polulation is used, the probability of the sample represents the whole population as well

Common wording:

  • Which conclusion is supported?
  • Based on the sample...
  • What value most likely represents the probability of the whole population?
  • Which estimate is most reasonable?

Common traps:

  • Assume sample results are exact (no margin of error)
  • Misidentifying the who is represented in the whole population
  • Make conclusions that go beyond what the data supports
Evaluating Statistical Claims

What to look for:

  • Claims based on data
  • Experimental design or sample selections
  • Bias, random samples, or cause-and-effect conclusions

Strategies:

  • Consider sample size. Is the sample size too small or too large to predict accurate data?
  • Identify the sampling method (was it a random sample?)
  • Look for possible biases (are all groups represented in the sample?)

Common wording:

  • Which claim is best supported by the data?
  • Which statement can best be concluded from the study?

Common traps:

  • Overlooking non-random samples or other biases
  • Predicting an entire population from a small sample size
  • Choosing an answer that goes beyond what the evidence actually shows
